Thermal Management

Keeping crew, equipment, and habitat systems within safe temperature ranges despite Martian diurnal swings of up to 145°C between day and night.

04.1

Why the swings are so extreme

Mars's thin CO₂ atmosphere holds almost none of the heat-buffering capacity Earth's atmosphere and oceans provide. Surface temperature can swing from around 20°C at midday near the equator in summer to below −125°C at the poles in winter, and even a single equatorial sol can see a swing of 80°C or more between midday and predawn. Landed missions confirm this directly: NASA's InSight lander recorded nighttime lows near −95°C at its mid-latitude site.

04.2

Insulation and heating

Habitat hulls use high-performance insulation, following the precedent of aerogel blankets used for thermal protection on the Mars Exploration Rovers and subsequent missions — aerogel provides very low thermal conductivity at a fraction of the mass of traditional insulation. Where continuous low-power heat is needed for sensitive components, radioisotope heater units (RHUs) provide steady output independent of sunlight, as used on multiple Mars rover missions; crewed habitats supplement this with resistive electric heating drawing on the surface power system, and closed-loop fluid heat-transport systems to move waste heat from equipment to where it is needed.

Hazards

Cold-soak equipment failure — batteries and hydraulics lose function sharply below their rated range

Unheated equipment left outside overnight can cold-soak below its operating range within hours, risking battery damage and material embrittlement. All EVA and surface equipment requires a heating plan before nightfall. See related systems: habitat structure and communications.
