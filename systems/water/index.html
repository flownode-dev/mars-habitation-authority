Water Extraction

Sourcing water from polar ice, subsurface ice, and hydrated regolith, and recovering it in a closed-loop recycling system once inside the habitat.

02.1

Where the water is

Mars holds substantial water, almost entirely as ice. The polar caps contain water ice layered with seasonal CO₂ ice. Orbital neutron-spectrometer surveys and radar sounding (Mars Odyssey, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter) have mapped extensive subsurface ice at mid-to-high latitudes, in places within a meter of the surface. NASA's Phoenix lander directly confirmed and sampled subsurface water ice near 68°N in 2008. Away from ice deposits, regolith across much of the planet is measurably hydrated, typically holding on the order of 1–3% water by mass bound in minerals, as measured by the Curiosity rover.

02.2

Extraction and recycling

Ice-rich regolith is heated to sublime or melt water directly (thermal extraction), or excavated and processed in enclosed ovens. Hydrated regolith requires higher processing temperatures to release chemically bound water. Once inside the habitat, water is not a consumable — it is a closed loop. The International Space Station's water recovery system, the closest operational precedent, recovers on the order of 90–98% of water from crew urine, sweat, and cabin humidity for reuse. A Mars habitat's closed-loop target follows the same principle: recycle drinking water, water used for oxygen electrolysis, and water used in radiation-shielding walls, with makeup water drawn from local ice only to cover irrecoverable losses.

Hazards

Perchlorate-contaminated ice and regolith — treat all extracted water before consumption

Perchlorate salts are present in Martian regolith and can co-occur with ice deposits at concentrations toxic to humans. Extracted water must pass through perchlorate-reduction treatment before use as drinking water. See related systems: atmosphere & oxygen and food production.
