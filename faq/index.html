Frequently Asked Questions

Plain-language answers. For technical detail, see the relevant system reference.

Can I breathe the Martian air?

No. The atmosphere is 95.3% carbon dioxide at roughly 0.6% of Earth's surface pressure. Unprotected exposure causes loss of consciousness within seconds and is fatal within minutes. All breathing air is manufactured and contained within pressurized habitats, suits, and vehicles. See Atmosphere & Oxygen.

How long does it take to get to Mars?

Using a standard low-energy transfer trajectory, roughly six to nine months one-way, depending on the specific launch date and trajectory chosen within a given transfer window. See Gravity & Health for what that transit time means physiologically.

Can I drink the water on Mars?

Not directly. Local water sources — polar ice, subsurface ice, hydrated regolith — require extraction and perchlorate-removal treatment before they are safe to drink. See Water Extraction.

Can plants grow directly in Martian soil?

Not reliably, and not without treatment. Martian regolith lacks organic material and contains perchlorate salts toxic to most crops. The current baseline is controlled-environment hydroponic and aeroponic agriculture rather than open regolith farming. See Food Production.

Will I feel lighter on Mars?

Yes. Martian gravity is 3.72 m/s², about 38% of Earth's. The physiological effects of sustained partial gravity are not yet fully characterized from direct human experience, but are expected to fall somewhere between full gravity and the well-documented effects of microgravity. See Gravity & Health.

Can I talk to Earth in real time?

No. One-way radio delay runs from about 3 to 22 minutes depending on the current distance between Earth and Mars, so a conversation with a reply is at minimum a 6-minute round trip, and can be much longer. During solar conjunction, roughly every 26 months, there is no contact with Earth at all for about two weeks. See Communications.

What happens in an emergency if Earth can't help in time?

Surface operations are designed to be handled autonomously by onsite crew and protocol, precisely because Earth cannot respond in real time. See the hazard reference and Communications.

How many people currently live on Mars?

Zero. See the note at the bottom of every page on this site. This manual is maintained as a standing reference in advance of need, not as a record of an active settlement.
